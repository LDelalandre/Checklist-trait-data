Appendix S2 - Operational Checklist

This operational checklist follows the same structure as the detailed checklist and the article. It can be stored in your project folder, and allows you to tick the actions that you already performed. Provided you do not move the file, the ticked items will be saved, and will still be ticked when reopening the file. NB: for the revision, this document is presented as a pdf, but the interactive HTML document is available on GitHub: https://htmlpreview.github.io/?https://github.com/Ldelalandre/Checklist-trait-data/blob/main/Appendix%20S2%20-%20Operational%20checklist.html.

A) MAKE TRAIT DATA MEANINGFUL

A.1) Document the trait

▸
Why this matters: The E_Q formalism allows one to identify unequivocally the trait measured (semantic disambiguation).
▸
Why this matters: Improper sampling protocol can produce erroneous results and limit the value of data. Different sampling protocols for the same trait are likely to yield different values. Follow recommendations in handbooks when available.
▸
Why this matters: Allows multiple trait measurements to be correctly linked to the same sample (e.g., Leaf Area and Leaf Mass measured on the same leaf).
▸
Why this matters: Improper measurement protocol can produce erroneous results and limit the value of data. Different measurement protocols for the same trait are likely to yield different values. When available, standardized protocols should be used (e.g. reference handbooks).
▸
Why this matters: Adhering to domain-relevant community standards is a key step to ensure (i) quality measurement, (ii) reproducibility, and (iii) interoperabilty and reusability. Note that some questions might require the collection of data that do not comply to existing standards; in this case, detailed information should be given so that trait data can be properly interpreted.
▸
Why this matters: Needed to distinguish replicated measurements of the same trait on the same sample.
▸
Why this matters: The decision as to which aggregation level is relevant (e.g., population or species) should be left to the end users, allowing them to assemble appropriate data for their specific research questions. This places the responsibility of recording, storing, and publishing the finest trait value resolution possible on the data creator.
▸
Why this matters: Unit is one of the minimal, essential features required to qualify continuous trait data, making data analyses possible. Units do not apply to categorical data.
▸
Why this matters: Enables one to keep track of the measurements made.
▸
Why this matters: Needed to connect the measurements to an ecological context (if made in the field).

A.2) Document the sampled organism

▸
Why this matters: Provides a standardized identifier of the organism, ensuring clarity and avoiding confusion due to vernacular names.
▸
Why this matters: Ensures taxonomic precision, especially for species with homonyms or name changes
▸
Why this matters: Ensures consistency with current nomenclature and enables linkage to global taxonomic backbones.
▸
Why this matters: Provides stable, unique links across databases, facilitating interoperability and data reuse (B.1.2), and enables to uniquely identify the taxon within the database (B.2.4).
▸
Why this matters: Helps trace historical data and avoid loss of information when names change.
▸
Why this matters: Captures meaningful variation below the species level
▸
Why this matters: Facilitates data integration and comparative analyses across broader taxonomic groups.
▸
Why this matters: Characteristics of the taxon, often reported in floras or taxonomic databases, are crucial for interpreting trait values, understanding their variation, and enabling correct cross-species comparisons.
▸
Why this matters: The physiological or phenological state of an individual (e.g., its developmental stage, health, or sex) can cause significant intra-specific variation in trait measurements. Documenting this context is essential for correctly interpreting the data and for reusing it in studies of phenotypic plasticity or life history.

A.3) Document the environment

▸
Why this matters: Trait variations can occur at small spatial scales, part of which can be understood if the spatial organisation of the studied system is described.
▸
Why this matters: Needed to make sense of the variables. One of the most commonly reported variable is geolocation.
▸
Why this matters: Different sampling protocols for the same variable are likely to yield different values.
▸
Why this matters: Different measurement methods for the same variable are likely to yield different values.
▸
Why this matters: Unit is one of the minimal, essential features required to qualify continuous data, making data analyses possible.
▸
Why this matters: References to ontologies or thesaurus, or clear definitions (sampling protocol, measurement method, etc.) are needed to be able to use these measurements.
▸
Why this matters: Environmental measurements can be performed at different spatial scales, from the local proximity of the plant to the site or the climatic region. The scale of the measurement must be clearly reported.
▸
Why this matters: While measuring environmental variables locally is preferable, when these are not available, is it recommended to share environmental measures extracted from databases if they are relevant to understand and analyse trait data.

A.4) Document the project

▸
Why this matters: Systematic error can differ among experimenters. This information can be used to control for data quality.
▸
Why this matters: Necessary for any question on the data.
▸
Why this matters: Documenting origin is necessary to avoid the same dataset, shared on different platforms, to be considered as two distinct datasets (risk of pseudo-replication in further analyses).

B) MAKE TRAIT DATA INTEROPERABLE

B.1) Format and structure the data

▸
Why this matters: Necessary to ensure interoperability
▸
Why this matters: Creates a direct connexion between trait data and their contextual data, which makes the data table more "self-sufficient", and hence more interoperable.
▸
Why this matters: Depending on the heterogeneity of the data, one can decide to keep the structure used for data collection and/or data analysis, or restructure data for sharing them. Data restructuration often enables one to include more thorough data descriptions within the data table, thus connecting them directly to the data points. Direct understanding the data points contained in tables is key for data interoperability.
▸
Why this matters: Unique identifiers are necessary to uniquely identify measurements within each table
▸
Why this matters: Referring, in the core observation table, to the primary key of each extension, is necessary to connect this core table to its extensions.

B.2) Standardize variable names

▸
Why this matters: Following a data standard with unambiguous field (i.e., column) names makes data structuration easier to understand by data reusers and to combine (manually or automatically) to other data, which are determinant contributions to the interoperability of datasets.
▸
Why this matters: Standards might not be adapted to all data, in particular when data are very original in their content.

B.3) Standardize variable content

▸
Why this matters: This practice is fundamental for interoperability, allowing different datasets to be unambiguously combined and understood by automated systems. Examples of ontologies include the Trait Ontology (TO) for traits, or the Methods Ontology for procedures.
▸
Why this matters: Ensures correct species identification and links to global taxonomic resources. This is necessary for combining different data sets, which may name the same taxa differently.
▸
Why this matters: Allows for the integration of environmental context across different studies.

B.4) Ensure reproducible data processing

▸
Why this matters: Keeps raw data intact and ensures all transformations are explicit, traceable, and reproducible.
▸
Why this matters: Versioning of the scripts makes it possible to go back to previous versions of the data restructuration process if necessary.
▸
Why this matters: Sharing the code ensures the data processing is transparent, reproducible, and allows others to build upon your methodology.
▸
Why this matters: Needed to explicitly state the conditions under which the scripts can be reused/modified.

C) MAKE TRAIT DATA FINDABLE

C.1) Publish a metadata file in a standard format

▸
Why this matters: Using a metadata standard (1) ensures that all key aspects of the metadata are effectively documented, and (2) favours the interoperability with other datasets documented using the same metadata standard. A best practice is to adopt widely recognized detailed metadata standards, such as EML or ISO 19115 for ecological data.
▸
Why this matters: Metadata are the proxy by which data are searched.

C.2) Publish a rich metadata file

▸
Why this matters: Necessary to have a general glimpse of the dataset, and for querying with search engines.
▸
Why this matters: Metadata must document the exact structuration of the data, generally into tables, and describe each field (i.e., column name) of the tables, for the users to be able navigate the data.
▸
Why this matters: Quality control is necessary to confidently use data. Details of how this control was made can further guide data analysis. This information can thus orient data search.
▸
Why this matters: This accessibility information (the 'where' and 'how') must be recorded in the metadata itself to enable discovery and access.

C.3) Promote the dataset

▸
Why this matters: Using multiple channels to inform on data publication enhances its findability.

D) MAKE TRAIT DATA ACCESSIBLE

D.1) Preserve and license the data package

▸
Why this matters: Ensures that data will be available on the long-term.
▸
Why this matters: Metadata are necessary to make sense of the data (see above).
▸
Why this matters: A unique and persistent identifier is necessary for the data to be findable.
▸
Why this matters: Necessary for data reuse.

D.2) Preserve and license the processing scripts

▸
Why this matters: Srcipts inform on the procedures implemented to control data and to make it interoperable.

E) PERFORM QUALITY CONTROLS (QCs)

E.1) QC of dataset Description (step 1)

▸
Why this matters: Consistency of the data structure is necessary to reuse the data. Controlling that the row number is as expected helps identifying (partial) duplications. When data is structured in different tables, their correspondence must be verified, i.e., the common columns between the tables must contain the same information.
▸
Why this matters: Outlier detection allows one to detect values that are very different from the other values of the data distribution. They may reveal (1) errors easy to correct (e.g., unit error, wrongly placed decimal); (2) biological variability to be kept in the dataset, or (3) an error which requires removing the data point.
▸
Why this matters: Divergent units for the same trait across subparts of datasets can be an important source of errors.
▸
Why this matters: Non-numeric variables (e.g., species name, individual identifier, etc.) are used to connect data points together (e.g., measurements made on the same species, or trait and environmental measurements). They must be carefully checked for homogeneity across the database, as small mispelling errors can prevent connecting these data points.
▸
Why this matters: Functional links are a prerequisite for accessing the external resources cited in the database.
▸
Why this matters: Many errors can occur when correcting data values and restructuring data. It is thus absolutely necessary to keep track of which changes were made, and to be able to go back to previous versions if these corrections turn out to be irrelevant.

E.2) QC of dataset Publication (step 2)

▸
Why this matters: Metadata is the first access to a dataset, and must thus be exhaustive and correct so that potential data reusers have an accurate assessment of the dataset content, structure, and accessibility.
▸
Why this matters: Ensures that the correct, final version of all files (data, metadata, scripts) has been uploaded and that no errors occurred during the publication process. Additionally ensures that two elements of paramount importance for accessibility (DOIs and license) are present.
